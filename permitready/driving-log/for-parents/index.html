A Parent's Guide to Supervising Your Teen Driver

What you are responsible for, how to make practice sessions count, and how to keep a log you can sign your name to.

TechSmog Apps › PermitReady › Supervised Driving Hours Log › For Parents

Your job as the supervising adult comes down to three things: be a qualifying supervisor under your state's rules, be in the car for the hours you certify, and sign an accurate record at the end. Everything else — how you structure practice, what conditions you cover, whether you use paper or an app — is up to you, and this page is about doing that part well.

What you are legally responsible for

Every state runs a graduated driver licensing (GDL) program, and every one of them puts an adult in the passenger seat for a reason. The specifics vary, but the responsibilities are consistent.

1. Being a qualifying supervisor

Most states require a licensed driver at least 21 years old in the front passenger seat. A handful set the bar higher — Delaware, Indiana, New Hampshire and Vermont look for 25 — and several add an experience floor on top of the age: Washington asks for five years of licensure, Maryland three, New Jersey three, Nevada and Illinois one. A parent almost always qualifies, but a 22-year-old sibling might not, depending on where you live.

2. Meeting the hour requirement

Most states land between 40 and 60 total hours with a night minimum, commonly 10. The outliers are worth knowing about early rather than in month five: Oregon asks for 100 hours without driver education, Maine 70, Pennsylvania 65 (including five hours in poor weather), Kentucky, Maryland and North Carolina 60. New York, Minnesota, Louisiana and Virginia all want 15 night hours rather than 10.

3. Signing the certification

This is the part people underestimate. In most states a parent or guardian signs a document certifying the hours, and in several states that document is a sworn affidavit:

  • Ohio — the Fifty-Hour Affidavit (BMV 5791), signed under oath and submitted with the road test application.
  • Pennsylvania — form DL-180C, which asks for the total, the night hours, and the poor-weather hours as three separate numbers.
  • New York — form MV-262, Certification of Supervised Driving.
  • Massachusetts — the RMV Supervised Driving Log, signed by the parent, which the RMV may ask to see.
  • Florida and Texas — a parent-signed driving log presented at the road test.

A few states, California among them, do not mandate a specific form — the parent certifies on the license application and the DMV simply recommends keeping a log in any format. Either way, the signature is an attestation. Keep a record you would be comfortable defending.

4. The vehicle

Not a licensing rule, but worth handling before the first drive: call your insurer and tell them there is a permit holder in the household. Most carriers cover a permitted driver under the existing policy without a premium change until they are licensed, but the ones that do not are the ones you want to know about now. Confirm the car is registered and inspected, and decide which car is the practice car — consistency helps more than you would expect in the first month.

What counts as a supervised hour

Parents lose more hours to bad bookkeeping than to bad driving. The rules are simpler than the folklore:

The everyday driving counts. The trip to school. The run to the grocery store. Twenty minutes to a friend's house. If the permit holder is at the wheel and a qualifying supervisor is in the front passenger seat on a public road, that is a supervised hour whether or not it felt like a lesson.

  • Short trips count. A twelve-minute drive is twelve minutes. Four of those in a week is nearly an hour, and families who only count "real practice sessions" routinely undercount themselves by twenty or thirty hours over six months.
  • Parking lots usually count — most states count any driving time with a qualifying supervisor. But Pennsylvania and a few others take the position that empty lots do not serve the purpose of the requirement, and no state wants a log made mostly of lot time. Use them for the first few sessions and for specific skills, then get onto roads.
  • Night hours are counted separately and are the requirement families most often come up short on. See the timing section below.
  • Time parked, idling, or waiting does not count. Log driving time, not trip time. If you sat in a drive-through for fifteen minutes, that is not fifteen minutes of practice.
  • Hours before the permit was issued do not count. The permit has to be in hand.
  • Hours with a non-qualifying supervisor do not count even if the driving was fine.

How to make practice sessions actually productive

Start with one skill per session

Early on, a session with a single objective beats an hour of unfocused driving. "Today we are doing right turns and stop signs" is a better session than "let's just drive around." The teen has one thing to get better at and one thing to be corrected on, which is roughly the number of corrections anyone can absorb while also operating a car.

Give feedback after, not during

The single most useful habit: unless it is a safety issue that needs to be said now, hold the comment until the car is stopped. Running commentary from the passenger seat raises the stress level and lowers the amount of learning. Say the safety-critical thing immediately and calmly; save everything else for the driveway. Two minutes of "here's what went well, here's the one thing for next time" at the end of each drive is worth more than thirty corrections during it.

Vary the conditions deliberately

Most logs end up as fifty hours of the same six familiar roads in dry daylight, which produces a teen who is confident on those six roads. Rotate through:

  • Residential streets — the starting point, and where most of the first ten hours belong.
  • Multi-lane arterials — lane changes, turn lanes, timed lights.
  • Highway — merging, speed judgment, following distance at 65.
  • Rain — you cannot schedule it, so take it when it comes. Pennsylvania requires five poor-weather hours by name, and families who ignore weather until spring end up needing rain in a dry May.
  • Night — nearly every state's separate minimum.
  • Heavy traffic — a Saturday afternoon shopping district is a legitimate lesson.
  • Parking — angled, perpendicular, parallel, and backing out of a tight space with cars either side.

Time the night hours around the calendar

This is the piece of scheduling that saves families the most grief. Night hours are far easier to collect between October and February, when it is dark by 5 PM and a normal evening errand is a night hour. In June, "night driving" means keeping a fifteen-year-old out past 9:30 PM on a school night, repeatedly.

Two complications to plan around. First, several states define night by the clock rather than by darkness — California counts 11 PM to 5 AM, and Nebraska, New York and Utah count sunset to sunrise. If your state uses a clock definition, an 8 PM December drive may not qualify. Second, many permits carry a night curfew of their own; Pennsylvania permit holders cannot drive between midnight and 5 AM, which leaves a narrow window. Check both before you plan.

Introduce the highway on your terms

Do not let the first highway drive be an accident of the route. Pick the moment: after the teen is comfortable with lane changes and speed control on a 45 mph road, typically somewhere between hours 15 and 25. Then structure it — a quiet on-ramp at an off-peak hour, one exit, off, turn around, one exit back. Drive the route yourself first if you can and talk through where the merge point is. Fifteen minutes of well-chosen highway beats an hour of white-knuckled improvisation, and the second highway drive is always easier than the first.

Keep sessions short and frequent

Three thirty-minute drives across a week beat one two-hour marathon. Concentration degrades, and the last forty minutes of a long session usually teach nobody anything. Frequent short drives also make the everyday trips — school, practice, the store — feel like part of the plan rather than an interruption to it.

A realistic six-month plan

Month 1 — Control

Roughly 5–8 hours

Empty lots and quiet residential streets. Steering, braking smoothly, mirrors, signaling, stop signs, right turns before left turns. Short sessions, 20–30 minutes. Expect this month to feel slow.

Months 2–3 — Real roads

Roughly 20–25 hours

Busier streets, multi-lane roads, left turns across traffic, four-way stops, lane changes, parking practice. Start banking night hours if the season allows. Introduce the highway near the end of this stretch.

Months 4–5 — Conditions

Roughly 20 hours

Highway at speed, rush hour, rain whenever it happens, night driving in volume, unfamiliar routes where the teen navigates. This is where a driver stops being a student and starts being a driver.

Month 6 — Road test shape

Remaining hours

Parallel parking, three-point turns, backing in a straight line, and the specific maneuvers your state tests. Drive near the test site. Verify the log total against the requirement and get the certification form filled in before the appointment, not at it.

Fifty hours over six months is about two hours a week. It sounds like a lot in month one and like nothing by month four.

Frequently asked questions

Can someone who isn't a parent supervise driving hours?

In most states, yes. The usual rule is a licensed driver over a set age — 21 in the majority of states, 25 in Delaware, Indiana, New Hampshire and California's general case — seated in the front passenger seat. Several states add an experience requirement (Washington asks for five years of licensure, Maryland three, Illinois one). A few states are narrower and expect a parent, guardian, or a specific adult the parent has authorized. Check who qualifies as a supervisor in your state before you count an hour someone else supervised.

What happens if we move to another state partway through the permit period?

Contact the new state's licensing agency before you assume anything. States generally do not have a formal transfer process for logged hours, but in practice most will accept an accurate record from the previous state as long as the hours were supervised by a qualifying driver. What is far less portable is the permit itself and the holding period — some states restart the clock, some credit time already served. The safest approach is to keep logging without a gap, keep the record detailed, and ask the new agency in writing what they will credit.

Do driving school hours count toward the parent-supervised requirement?

Usually not — they are typically counted separately. California's six professional behind-the-wheel hours are in addition to the 50 supervised hours, and Ohio's eight instructor hours are separate from the 50. What driver education often does instead is reduce the total: Oregon drops from 100 hours to 50 with an approved course, Massachusetts from 40 to 30 with a certified skills program, and Texas allows as few as 10 behind-the-wheel hours through an approved course. Read your state's rule carefully, because "counts toward" and "reduces the requirement" are different things.

Do I have to be in the car for every hour?

You have to be in the car for every hour you certify. If another qualifying adult supervised a drive, that drive still counts toward the state's requirement, but you are signing a statement about hours you did not personally witness. Most parents handle this by having the other supervisor tell them the date and duration and logging it with a note. Keep the note — it is the difference between a record and a guess.

What am I actually signing when I certify the hours?

In most states, a sworn or affirmed statement. Ohio's Fifty-Hour Affidavit is signed under oath. Pennsylvania's DL-180C asks for three separate numbers — total, night, and poor weather. New York's MV-262 is a certification of supervised driving. These are legal attestations, and falsifying one is a false statement to a government agency. That is the real reason to keep an honest running log rather than reconstructing a plausible number the week before the road test.

Does time in a parking lot count?

It depends on the state, and it is the single most common point of confusion. Most states count any time the permit holder is driving with a qualifying supervisor, which includes a parking lot. Some states — Pennsylvania is explicit about this — take the view that the requirement exists to build real-traffic experience and that empty lots and closed courses do not serve it. When in doubt, log the drive with a note about where it happened and do not lean on lot hours to make up a shortfall.

How do I keep the log if my teen has the app on their phone?

Have them send you a copy. PermitReady exports the log as a file through the normal iOS share sheet — AirDrop when you are in the same room, Messages or Mail when you are not.
