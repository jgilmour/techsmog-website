Driving Log Template

What a good log entry actually contains, where to get a printable sheet, and when to use your state's form instead.

TechSmog Apps › PermitReady › Guides › Driving Log Template

A good log entry answers four questions: when, how long, in what conditions, and with whom. Anything that captures those four accurately is a valid record in most states. What makes a log fail is not its format — it is being filled in from memory weeks later.

What a good log entry looks like

Here are the same drive recorded three ways. All three are fine. The third is the one that is still useful in April.

Bare minimum 14/02 45 min night
Standard Date: 14 Feb 2026 Start 6:15 PM — End 7:00 PM (45 min) Conditions: night, city streets, rain Supervisor: Mom
Complete Date: 14 Feb 2026 Start 6:15 PM — End 7:00 PM (45 min) Conditions: night, city streets, rain Supervisor: Mom (licensed 18 yrs) Notes: Left turns across traffic on Main St. Much better than last week. Still braking late in the wet — work on following distance next time. Running total: 31.5 hrs | Night total: 8.0 hrs

The extra detail costs about ten seconds. What it buys: the next session has an objective, the night subtotal is always current, and if anyone ever questions a number you can say exactly where it came from.

The fields, and why each one is there

FieldRequired?Why
DateAlwaysHours only count once the permit is issued, and states certify hours accumulated during the permit period.
Start and end timeOftenSeveral official forms have columns for both. They also make a duration harder to inflate — which is rather the point.
DurationAlwaysDriving time, not trip time. Twenty minutes parked outside a shop is not practice.
Day or nightAlmost alwaysNearly every state sets a separate night minimum, and it is the number families come up short on.
ConditionsSometimesPennsylvania requires poor-weather hours by name; South Dakota and Alaska build conditions into the requirement. Everywhere else it shows you what you have been avoiding.
SupervisorSometimesMatters when several adults drive with the teen, and matters a lot to the parent signing a certification covering hours they did not personally witness.
NotesNeverFree, and the difference between a column of numbers and a training record. "First time merging." "Parallel parking, still rough."
Running totalEffectivelyEvery certification asks for a total. Keeping it current means never reconstructing it.

If you're building your own

  • One row per drive, not one row per day. Three short trips are three entries.
  • A separate night column, not a note in the conditions field. It is a number your state asks for on its own.
  • Running totals at the bottom of every page, carried forward. A log you have to add up at the end is a log that gets added up wrong.
  • Header fields for the driver's name, permit number, and permit issue date. The issue date is the one people forget and then need.
  • A certification line for the parent or guardian, with a date. Even if your state has its own form, having the sentence in front of you is a useful reminder of what the signature means.
  • Room to write. A log with 3 mm rows does not get filled in.

Habits that keep a template filled in

  • Fill it in in the driveway. Before the seatbelt comes off. Every minute you wait costs accuracy.
  • One person owns the log. Two people casually logging the same drives is how totals get double-counted.
  • Log the boring trips. School runs and errands are the majority of your hours.
  • Check the night subtotal monthly. It falls behind quietly while the total looks healthy.
  • Never round up to a milestone. Forty-seven hours is forty-seven hours — and in several states the parent's signature on the total is a sworn statement.

Frequently asked questions

What should a driving log entry include?

Date, start and end time or a duration, the conditions that applied (day, night, highway, rain, city, parking, heavy traffic), and who supervised. A one-line note about what you practiced is optional and worth more than it costs. The first three are what states ask about; the last two are what make the log useful to you rather than just to a clerk.

Is there an official driving log template?

It depends on the state. Ohio publishes the Fifty-Hour Affidavit (BMV 5791), Pennsylvania form DL-180C, New York form MV-262, Massachusetts an RMV Supervised Driving Log, and Florida and Texas both publish log formats. California requires no particular form and simply recommends keeping a log. Where a state publishes a form, use theirs — a generic template is a running record you copy onto it, not a replacement for it.

Can I make my own driving log?

In most states, yes, as long as it is accurate and captures what the state asks about — which almost always means a total and a night subtotal. A spreadsheet, a notebook, a printed template, or an app all work. The format matters far less than whether it was filled in at the time. What you cannot do is substitute your own document for a state form where one is mandated.

Does the parent have to sign every entry?

Usually not every line, though some official forms have a per-drive signature column and a few families use one as a discipline. What states almost universally require is a single parent or guardian certification of the total at the end, and in several states that certification is sworn. Signing per drive is a stricter habit than required; signing nothing until the last day is how totals become guesses.

What if I made a mistake in the log?

Correct it visibly rather than invisibly. On paper, strike through, write the correction, and initial it — an erased or overwritten entry looks worse than an obvious correction. In an app, edit the entry. Either way, if the correction is significant, note why: "duplicate of 14 Feb entry, removed". A log with visible corrections reads as a real record; a spotless one that nobody could have kept perfectly does not.

Should I use paper or an app?

Most families are better off with an app, mainly because the phone is in the car and the sheet is in the other car, and because a digital log totals itself. Paper wins when your state mandates a specific printed form or when the teen does not carry a phone. Plenty of families do both: log on the phone as each drive ends, transcribe onto the state form once at the end.
