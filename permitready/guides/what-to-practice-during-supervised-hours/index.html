What to Practice During Supervised Driving Hours

A checklist by phase — control, traffic, conditions — so fifty hours produce a driver rather than fifty hours on the same six roads.

TechSmog Apps › PermitReady › Guides › What to Practice During Supervised Hours

Work in three phases: control, traffic, then conditions. Each phase has a small number of things to get right before the next one makes sense. Most families instead drive the same familiar loop fifty times and end up with a teen who is confident on six roads and nowhere else. This page is the list to work through so that doesn't happen.

The hours themselves are not the point — a state asking for 50 hours is trying to buy experience across a range of situations. The log tells you how much time you have spent; only a checklist tells you what you have actually covered. Pair this with your state's requirement from the hours-by-state table.

Phase 1 — Control (roughly hours 1–10)

Empty parking lots and quiet residential streets. Sessions of 20–30 minutes. Nothing to hit and nothing to hurry for.

  • Cockpit setup — seat position, mirrors set properly (blind spots minimized, not showing the side of your own car), head restraint, steering wheel position, seatbelt. Every single time, until it is automatic.
  • Smooth starting and stopping — the goal is a passenger who does not notice the stop. Brake early and ease off at the end.
  • Steering technique — hand position, feeding the wheel, and looking where you want to go rather than at the hood.
  • Speed control without watching the speedometer — holding 25 mph by feel, then checking.
  • Signaling every time, including in an empty parking lot. Habits form now or not at all.
  • Mirror scanning on a rhythm — every 5–8 seconds, and before every speed or position change.
  • Right turns before left turns. Right turns are one decision; left turns are three.
  • Stop signs — stopping behind the line, then creeping to see, then going. The two-stage stop is what examiners look for and what teens skip.
  • Reversing in a straight line, looking over the shoulder rather than only at the camera.
  • Basic parking — perpendicular in and out, with cars either side once the empty version is easy.

Ready for phase 2 when: they can drive a quiet residential loop for fifteen minutes without a correction, and the controls have stopped requiring conscious thought.

Phase 2 — Traffic (roughly hours 10–30)

Real roads with other cars on them. This is the bulk of the requirement and where most of the actual learning happens.

  • Left turns across traffic — unprotected, at a light, at a stop sign. Judging an oncoming gap is the single hardest early skill.
  • Four-way stops and who goes when, including the case where everyone hesitates.
  • Multi-lane roads — choosing a lane early, turn lanes, and not drifting during a shoulder check.
  • Lane changes on a fixed routine: mirror, signal, shoulder check, move, cancel.
  • Roundabouts, if your area has them. Yield on entry, signal on exit, do not stop inside.
  • Following distance — the three-second rule, counted out loud a few times until it becomes a feel.
  • Traffic lights — the stop-or-go decision on a yellow, and not entering an intersection you cannot clear.
  • Pedestrians, cyclists, and school zones — crosswalk rules, scanning between parked cars, passing a cyclist with room.
  • Lane discipline near large vehicles — staying out of blind spots and giving trucks room to turn.
  • Railway crossings, school buses, and emergency vehicles — the rules that appear on the permit test and almost never in practice until the day they do.
  • Navigating — let the teen decide the route sometimes. Driving while thinking about where you are going is a separate skill from driving.
  • Start banking night hours if the season allows. See what your state requires →

Ready for phase 3 when: a 45 mph multi-lane road is unremarkable, lane changes are routine, and left turns no longer produce a long pause.

Phase 3 — Conditions (roughly hours 30–50+)

Same skills, harder circumstances. This is where a student becomes a driver, and it is the phase families skip because the log total already looks respectable.

  • Highway driving — merging at speed, maintaining pace with traffic, exiting, and the fact that at 65 mph everything happens sooner than it looks.
  • Night driving — headlight glare, judging distance with no depth cues, unlit rural roads, animals at the roadside, and reading signs late. Nearly every state's separate minimum.
  • Rain — longer braking distances, hydroplaning, wipers and defoggers found without looking, and the first ten minutes of rain being the slipperiest.
  • Snow and ice, where you live with them. Gentle inputs, much longer distances, and what a skid feels like before it matters.
  • Heavy traffic — a Saturday afternoon shopping district or a genuine rush hour. Stop-and-go, merging when nobody is letting you in, patience.
  • Unfamiliar routes — somewhere neither of you drives regularly, with the teen navigating.
  • Parallel parking, properly, on a real street with real cars. The maneuver most likely to fail a road test.
  • Hills — starting on a slope, parking with wheels turned correctly, and engine braking on a long descent.
  • Rural roads — no lighting, no shoulder, sharp bends, farm vehicles, and passing decisions.
  • Longer drives — an hour or more, where fatigue and concentration become the lesson.
  • Emergencies, discussed and where safe practiced — a blowout, a warning light, brakes that feel wrong, what to do when a car will not restart on a shoulder. Also: where the spare, jack, and hazard switch are.

Road test preparation (the last few hours)

The road test examines a narrow set of things, and it is worth aiming at them directly in the final stretch:

  • The maneuvers your state tests — commonly parallel parking, a three-point turn, reversing in a straight line, and hill parking. Ask your agency for the scoring sheet; several publish one.
  • Observation the examiner can see — visible head turns at intersections, mirror checks before braking, shoulder checks on every lane change. Examiners mark what they can observe.
  • Full stops behind the line. A rolling stop is the most common automatic fault in the country.
  • Speed limits, exactly. Under the limit is marked too.
  • The roads around the test center. Drive them a few times. Test routes are rarely a secret.
  • The vehicle itself — working lights, turn signals, horn, wipers, and a windshield that is not cracked. Tests get canceled at the curb for this.

How to run a session so it works

  • One objective per drive. "Today we are doing left turns" produces more improvement than an hour of general driving, because one correction is roughly what anyone can absorb while also operating a car.
  • Brief before, debrief after. Thirty seconds at the start on what you are working on; two minutes at the end on one thing that went well and one thing for next time.
  • Feedback after, not during. Unless it is a safety issue, hold it until the car is stopped. Running commentary raises stress and lowers learning.
  • Short and frequent beats long and rare. Three thirty-minute drives across a week beat one two-hour session.
  • Count the ordinary trips. School runs and errands are real practice and real logged hours — families who only count "lessons" undercount themselves by twenty or thirty hours.
  • Log it in the driveway, with the conditions tagged, while it is still accurate.

Frequently asked questions

What should a new driver practice first?

Vehicle control, in a place with nothing to hit. Steering, smooth braking, mirror and seat setup, signaling, and the feel of the accelerator — usually in an empty parking lot, in twenty-minute sessions. Then quiet residential streets: stop signs, right turns, staying in the lane. Left turns across traffic and anything multi-lane can wait a few hours. The goal of the first month is a driver who does not have to think about the controls.

How many hours before the first highway drive?

Somewhere between 15 and 25 hours for most teens, and the marker is skill rather than the number: they should be comfortable holding a lane at 45 mph, changing lanes with a mirror-signal-shoulder-check routine that has become automatic, and judging gaps in moving traffic. Then structure the first one — a quiet on-ramp at an off-peak hour, one exit, off, turn around, one exit back. Fifteen well-chosen minutes beats an hour of improvisation.

What conditions do we have to cover?

Night is the one nearly every state requires by name, usually 10 hours. Pennsylvania also requires 5 hours in poor weather, and South Dakota and Alaska both build inclement or challenging conditions into their requirement. Beyond what is required, the list worth covering is: rain, night, highway, heavy traffic, unfamiliar routes, parking of all kinds, and whatever your region throws at you — snow and ice in the north, unlit rural roads, mountain grades, or flooded streets.

How long should a practice session be?

Twenty to forty minutes for the first month, up to an hour or so later. Three short drives across a week beat one long one: concentration degrades, and the last forty minutes of a marathon session usually teach nobody anything. Frequency also normalizes the everyday trips — school, practice, the store — as practice rather than interruptions.

How do I give feedback without making it worse?

Say the safety-critical thing immediately, calmly, in as few words as possible ("brake" beats a sentence). Save everything else for the driveway. Two minutes at the end — one thing that went well, one thing for next time — is worth more than thirty corrections during the drive, and it keeps the car from becoming a place where the teen expects to be criticised.

What should we practice right before the road test?

The specific maneuvers your state tests, plus the roads around the test site. Parallel parking, three-point turns, backing in a straight line, reverse into a space, hill parking where relevant, and the observation habits examiners score — mirror checks, head turns, full stops behind the line. Ask your agency for the scoring sheet if they publish one; several do, and it removes all guesswork about what is being marked.
