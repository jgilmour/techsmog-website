What the permit test actually is

The permit test — also called the knowledge test, written test, or learner's permit exam — is a multiple-choice exam drawn entirely from your state's official driver handbook. It is not a trick, it is not an aptitude test, and it is not trying to catch you out. It is checking that you have read the book.

Topics, in roughly the order they matter:

  • Road signs — regulatory, warning, guide, construction. Recognition by shape and colour as well as by content. A large share of most state tests.
  • Right of way — intersections, four-way stops, uncontrolled intersections, yielding to pedestrians and emergency vehicles, merging.
  • Traffic laws and signals — lights, lane markings, turn rules, U-turns, passing rules, school buses, railway crossings.
  • Speed and distance — posted and default limits, following distance, stopping distance, and how weather changes both.
  • Safe driving practices — scanning, blind spots, skids, hydroplaning, night driving, fatigue, distraction.
  • Alcohol and drugs — BAC limits (including the zero-tolerance limit for under-21 drivers), implied consent, and penalties. Almost always on the test.
  • State-specific numbers — fines, penalty points, insurance minimums, licence restrictions, curfews, passenger limits. These cannot be reasoned out, so they have to be learned.

Your state's format

There is no national permit test. Every state sets its own question count, pass mark, and rules:

  • Question count ranges from 18 (Pennsylvania) to 50 (Florida, Indiana, Michigan, New Jersey, Utah, Wisconsin). Twenty-five is the most common.
  • Pass mark is 80% in 37 states. New Mexico, New York and Texas set 70%; Massachusetts 72%; Maryland is the strictest at 88%, where 22 of 25 must be right.
  • Time limits are published by only a handful — Alaska and Massachusetts allow 25 minutes, Colorado, Florida, Hawaii and Texas an hour, DC 75 minutes. Most states publish none, which usually means time is not the constraint.
  • Split tests exist in Georgia, North Carolina, Texas, Virginia and Wisconsin, where signs and laws are scored as separate sections.
  • Minimum age ranges from 14 (Alaska, Arkansas, Iowa, Kansas, North Dakota, South Dakota) to 16 in eight states. Fifteen is the most common.

A study plan that works

Week 1 — Read the handbook

Two to three sessions, 30–45 minutes each

Download your state's official handbook — free from your licensing agency — and read it once, end to end, without trying to memorise. You are building a map, not learning it yet. Mark anything with a number in it: distances, ages, limits, fines, penalty points. Those pages get reread.

Week 2 — Questions, daily

15–20 minutes a day

Move to practice questions and do them every day rather than in one block. When you get one wrong, do not just note the right answer — go back to the handbook section it came from. A wrong answer is a pointer to a page you skimmed. Study by category if the tool allows it, and spend a whole session on road signs alone.

Week 3 — Full-length simulations

Until you're comfortably clear of the pass mark

Switch to full-length timed tests that match your state's question count and pass mark. Take several. If you are scoring in the mid-90s across a few different sets, you are ready. If you are hovering at the threshold, you are one unlucky question set from a retest — keep going for a few more days.

Techniques that actually help

  • Learn signs as a system. Octagon means stop, triangle means yield, yellow diamond means warning, orange means work zone, blue means services, brown means recreation. The system covers signs you have never seen.
  • Make the numbers a separate list. Every distance, age, limit, and fine on one sheet. These are the questions that cannot be reasoned out and the ones people fail on.
  • Read the whole question. A large share of wrong answers are right answers to a question that was not asked — particularly ones containing "except", "not", or "always".
  • Study in short sessions, daily. Twenty minutes a day for two weeks beats six hours the night before, by a wide margin and for well-understood reasons.
  • Explain a rule out loud to a parent. If you cannot explain why you yield, you do not know it yet.
  • Practise on the go. Waiting for a lift, sitting in a car park, five minutes before class. This is exactly why an offline study tool is worth more than a website.
  • Do not memorise answer positions. If you can recite that the answer is "C", you have learned the practice test rather than the material.

On test day

  • Bring the documents. Proof of identity, residency, Social Security number, parental consent where required, and the fee. Check your state's list the day before — being sent home for a missing document is the most common wasted trip.
  • Book an appointment if your state offers one. Walk-in waits can be long enough to matter.
  • Vision test first in most states. Bring your glasses or contacts, and expect a corrective-lens restriction on the permit if you need them.
  • Read every question fully before looking at the answers, and eliminate the obviously wrong ones.
  • Do not rush. Most states publish no time limit at all.
  • If you fail, ask about the retest rules before you leave — the waiting period, the fee, and whether anything else is required.

Frequently asked questions

How many questions are on the permit test?

It depends on your state, and the range is wide: Pennsylvania asks 18, New York and Vermont 20, Florida, Michigan, Indiana, New Jersey, Utah and Wisconsin 50. The most common format is 25 questions with an 80% pass mark, used by roughly a third of states. Thirty-seven states use 80% as the threshold; New Mexico, New York and Texas sit at 70%, while Maryland is the strictest at 88%.

What's on the permit test?

Everything in your state's official driver handbook, which in practice means road signs, right of way, traffic laws and signals, safe driving practices, speed limits and following distance, parking rules, alcohol and drug laws including implied consent, sharing the road with pedestrians, cyclists and trucks, and the state-specific numbers — fines, penalty points, BAC limits, and licence restrictions. The state-specific numbers are where people lose marks, because they cannot be reasoned out.

How long should I study for the permit test?

Two to three weeks of short daily sessions is realistic for most people, and considerably more effective than one long night. Read your state's handbook once end to end, then work through practice questions daily until you are scoring consistently above your state's pass mark with room to spare. If you are scoring 95% across several full-length practice tests, you are ready; if you are scraping the threshold, you are one bad question set away from a retest.

Can I take the permit test online?

Some states allow an online or at-home knowledge test under supervision, often through a driver education provider or with a proctor; many require it in person at a licensing office. This changes frequently and by state. Check your state's page and confirm with your licensing agency rather than assuming either way.

What happens if I fail?

Almost every state lets you retake it. What varies is the waiting period — anything from same-day to several days — the fee, and the number of attempts before something more is required. Failing is common and not a disaster. What matters is treating the failure as information: the questions you missed tell you exactly which handbook chapter you skimmed.

Are practice tests actually representative of the real exam?

They cover the same material in the same format, which is the useful part. Nobody outside a licensing agency has the actual test bank, so no practice app can promise the real questions — any that does is telling you something untrue. What good practice questions do is drill the same handbook content until recall is automatic, and expose the specific numbers and rules your state cares about.

Should I read the handbook or just do practice questions?

Both, in that order. Practice questions alone teach you to recognise answers you have seen before, which falls apart when the real exam words something differently. The handbook gives you the underlying rule; the questions turn it into recall and show you where the gaps are. Reading the handbook once and then living in the questions is the efficient sequence.

Do I need to study road signs separately?

Yes, and it is the highest-return hour of study you will do. Signs are a large share of most state tests, they are pure recognition rather than reasoning, and five states — Georgia, North Carolina, Texas, Virginia and Wisconsin — split the exam into a separate signs section and laws section, sometimes requiring a pass in each. Learn shape and colour as a system (octagon means stop, yellow diamond means warning, orange means work zone) and the individual signs get much easier.
