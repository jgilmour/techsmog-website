Supervised Driving Hours Required by State (2026)

Total hours, night hours, daily caps, and the driver-education shortcuts — all 50 states plus DC in one sortable table.

TechSmog Apps › PermitReady › Guides › Supervised Driving Hours by State

Most states require between 40 and 65 hours of supervised driving before a teen can apply for a full license. Roughly half of states land at 50 hours, and almost all require at least 10 of those hours to be at night. The outliers matter: Oregon asks for 100 hours without driver education, Maine 70, Pennsylvania 65, and Texas as few as 10 behind-the-wheel hours with an approved course.

What the spread actually tells you

Three patterns run through the table:

  • 50 is the default. More than half of jurisdictions settled on 50 total hours with 10 at night, which is roughly what the research on graduated licensing supports. If you are planning before you know your exact number, plan for 50/10 and adjust.
  • Driver education is the lever. The biggest reductions come from approved courses: Oregon halves 100 to 50, Massachusetts drops 40 to 30, Minnesota 50 to 40, and Texas allows the behind-the-wheel portion to shrink to 10 hours. In states where the course is optional, the arithmetic often favors taking it.
  • Night hours are the real constraint. Almost nobody fails to reach the total; plenty of families arrive at the road test short on night hours. Fifteen-hour states (New York, Maryland, Louisiana, Minnesota, Virginia) are the ones that bite, and California's 11 PM–5 AM definition is stricter than the sunset-to-sunrise rule everyone assumes.

How states define a "night" hour

This is the most common source of a rejected or re-done log:

  • Sunset to sunrise — the most common definition, used by New York and Nebraska among others. What counts shifts with the calendar, which makes winter the efficient time to bank night hours.
  • A fixed clock window — California counts 11 PM to 5 AM only. A 7 PM December drive is dark, and does not count.
  • "After sunset" — Utah's phrasing, effectively the same as sunset-to-sunrise for practical purposes.
  • "Darkness" — Wisconsin's wording, which follows actual conditions rather than a clock.

If your state's wording is ambiguous, log the actual clock time of every drive. A log with start and end times can be re-interpreted under any definition; a log that just says "night" cannot.

Conditions beyond night

A few states require specific conditions on top of the night minimum. Pennsylvania wants 5 of its 65 hours in bad weather. South Dakota wants 10 inclement-weather hours alongside 10 night hours. Alaska frames its 10 extra hours as "progressively challenging conditions," which includes both night and weather. North Dakota asks for practice in variable conditions. If you live somewhere it rains twice a year, these are worth planning around rather than hoping for.

Frequently asked questions

How many supervised driving hours do most states require?

Between 40 and 65, with about half of states at exactly 50. Almost all require at least 10 hours at night.

Which state requires the most hours?

Oregon, at 100 hours without an approved driver education course — halved to 50 with one. Maine (70) and Pennsylvania (65) come next.

Which state requires the fewest?

Texas, where an approved driver education course can bring the logged behind-the-wheel requirement down to 10 hours. Without driver education the figure is 30, still the lowest full requirement in the country. Adults 18 and over in Texas need no learner license at all.

How many night hours will I need?

Usually 10. New York, Maryland, Louisiana, Minnesota, and Virginia require 15; Georgia requires 6. Check how your state defines night before you assume an evening drive counts.

Can driver education reduce my required hours?

In several states. Massachusetts 40 to 30, Oregon 100 to 50, Minnesota 50 to 40, Texas down to 10 behind-the-wheel hours. Most states offer no reduction, and driver education is mandatory in some regardless.

Is there a limit on hours logged per day?

Ohio is the state most often cited for a daily cap, commonly around 4 hours. Most states publish none — but a reviewer who sees a 14-hour day in your log is entitled to be skeptical.
