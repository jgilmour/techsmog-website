Can a Friend Supervise Driving Hours?

Age, experience, and authorisation rules for supervising drivers in every state — and why the friend usually doesn't qualify.

TechSmog Apps › PermitReady › Guides › Can a Friend Supervise Driving Hours

Usually not. Most states require a licensed driver at least 21 years old in the front passenger seat, and six states — California, Delaware, Indiana, Missouri, New Hampshire and Vermont — set the bar at 25. Several also require years of licensed experience on top of the age. A teenager's friend qualifies almost nowhere; an older sibling, cousin, or family friend often does, provided they clear both the age and the experience test.

The three tests a supervisor has to pass

  1. Age. Twenty-one in most states, 25 in six, 20 in Connecticut and Maine, 18 in Montana, North Dakota and Wyoming. This is the test people check.
  2. Experience. Years of holding a licence, which is a separate hurdle: Washington asks for 5 years, Maryland, New Jersey and Oregon 3, Wisconsin 2, and Alaska, Illinois, Massachusetts, Montana, Nevada and South Carolina 1. A newly licensed 22-year-old can meet the age rule and fail this one. This is the test people forget.
  3. Relationship or authorisation, in some states. Several states restrict supervision to a parent, guardian, or an adult the parent has specifically authorised — Iowa, Michigan, Missouri and Virginia all word it this way. In those states, a qualifying adult still needs the parent's permission.

Everywhere, the supervisor sits in the front passenger seat. Several states say "in the seat beside the driver" precisely to remove the argument.

In every state listed, the supervising driver must be licensed and seated in the front passenger seat. Rules change — confirm the current requirement with your licensing agency before relying on a supervisor who is close to a threshold.

Does the supervisor need to own the car?

No. No state ties supervision to vehicle ownership — the requirements are about the licence, the age, the experience, and the seat. A teen can practise in a friend's car, a grandparent's car, or a car nobody in the household owns, and the hours count exactly the same.

Insurance is the real question hiding behind this one. Before practising in a car:

  • Tell your insurer there is a permit holder in the household. Most carriers cover a permitted driver under the existing policy without a premium change until they are licensed — but the ones that do not are the ones you want to discover now.
  • Check that the specific car is covered for a permit holder. Coverage usually follows the vehicle rather than the driver, so the owner's policy is the one that matters.
  • If you are borrowing a car, ask the owner to confirm with their insurer. "I assumed it was fine" is a bad position after a claim.

The mistakes that cost people hours

  • The 18-year-old friend. Qualifies in almost no state. Hours supervised this way do not count, and cannot honestly be certified.
  • The 22-year-old who just got licensed. Meets a 21 age rule, fails Washington's five-year rule and Oregon's and Maryland's three-year rule.
  • The qualifying adult nobody authorised. In Iowa, Michigan, Missouri and Virginia, a suitable adult still needs the parent's permission.
  • Supervising from the back seat with a younger passenger up front. Does not satisfy any state's rule.
  • Assuming a driving instructor's hours fold into the parent total. California's 6 professional hours and Ohio's 8 are additional, not included.
  • Assuming a sibling counts because they're family. Siblings get no special status in most states; grandparents sometimes do.

If some of your hours were supervised by someone who does not qualify, the answer is not to certify them anyway. Log what counts, note what does not, and drive the difference — in several states the parent's signature on the hour certification is a sworn statement.

Frequently asked questions

Can a friend supervise my driving hours?

Only if they meet your state's rule, and most friends of a teen driver do not. The typical requirement is a licensed driver at least 21 years old — and several states go further: California, Delaware, Indiana, Missouri, New Hampshire and Vermont look for 25. A number of states also require years of experience on top of the age: Washington 5 years, Maryland and New Jersey and Oregon 3, Wisconsin 2, and Alaska, Illinois, Massachusetts, Montana, Nevada and South Carolina 1. An 18-year-old friend qualifies almost nowhere.

Can a sibling supervise driving hours?

Same rule as anyone else — siblings get no special status in most states. A 22-year-old sister qualifies in a state with a 21 rule and does not in California, Delaware, Indiana, New Hampshire or Vermont. Watch the experience requirement too: a 22-year-old who was licensed at 21 fails Washington's five-year rule despite meeting the age.

Can a grandparent supervise?

Yes, provided they meet the state's age and experience rule, and some states name them explicitly. North Carolina lists a parent, grandparent, guardian or approved adult licensed at least 5 years, and Missouri names grandparents in its definition of a qualified instructor. Nothing about the relationship changes what the rest of the rules require.

Does the supervisor have to be the registered owner of the car?

No. No state ties the supervising-driver requirement to vehicle ownership — the rules are about the licence, the age, and the seat. What ownership does affect is insurance. Practising in a car that a household policy does not cover, or that lists no permitted driver, is an insurance problem rather than a licensing one, and it is worth a phone call to your insurer before it becomes a claim.

Where does the supervisor have to sit?

The front passenger seat, in essentially every state, and several word it as "in the seat beside the driver" specifically to close the argument. Supervising from the back seat does not satisfy the requirement, and hours logged that way are hours you cannot honestly certify.

Can a driving instructor's hours count toward the parent-supervised total?

Usually not — instructor hours are typically counted separately rather than folded in. California's 6 professional behind-the-wheel hours are additional to the 50 supervised hours, and Ohio's 8 instructor hours are separate from the 50. What driver education more often does is reduce the total requirement instead. Read your state's wording carefully, because "counts toward" and "reduces" are different rules.

What if a non-qualifying person supervised some of my hours?

Those hours do not count, however good the driving was. Do not certify them. The honest fix is to log the drives that qualify, note the ones that do not, and make up the difference — better to be twenty hours short and accurate than complete and wrong, since in several states the parent signs a sworn affidavit.

Do the same rules apply to adult learners over 18?

Often not. Graduated licensing requirements — hours, holding periods, and supervision rules — are generally written for applicants under 18, and adults typically face a shorter path. Texas states this explicitly. Whoever is in the car still has to be a licensed driver, and confirm your state's adult-applicant rules rather than assuming they mirror the teen ones.
