How to Log Supervised Driving Hours

What to record each session, paper versus app, and how to fix the log when you inevitably fall behind.

TechSmog Apps › PermitReady › Guides › How to Log Driving Hours

Log every drive the moment it ends, record the date, the duration, and whether it was at night, and never let the record become something you reconstruct from memory. That is the whole discipline. The rest of this page is about how to make it stick and what to do when it slips.

Why the log is the part that goes wrong

Almost nobody fails to accumulate the hours. Families drive their teens around constantly — school, practice, the store — and 50 hours over six months is about two hours a week. What goes wrong is the record. The glovebox sheet gets rained on, gets left in the other car, or gets filled in the night before the road test from a mixture of memory and hope.

That matters more than it used to, because most states now ask a parent to sign a certification: Ohio's Fifty-Hour Affidavit is sworn, Pennsylvania's DL-180C asks for three separate numbers, New York's MV-262 is a formal certification. Signing an attestation about hours you are guessing at is a bad position to be in, and it is entirely avoidable.

What to record for each session

Six fields. The first three are the ones states care about; the last three are the ones that make the log useful to you.

FieldWhy it matters
DateEvery state's certification is about hours accumulated during the permit period. Hours before the permit was issued do not count.
DurationDriving time, not trip time. Twenty minutes parked outside a store is not twenty minutes of practice.
Night or notNearly every state sets a separate night minimum, and it is the one families come up short on. Tag it at the time; you will not remember in April whether a February drive ended before or after dark.
ConditionsHighway, rain, city streets, parking, heavy traffic. Pennsylvania requires poor-weather hours by name. Everywhere else, condition tags show you what you have been avoiding.
SupervisorMatters when more than one adult supervises, and matters a lot if a state ever asks who was in the car. It also tells the certifying parent which hours they personally witnessed.
A one-line note"First time merging," "parallel parking, still rough," "rained the whole way home." Ten seconds now; the difference between a log and a training record later.

Here is what a good entry actually looks like:

14 Feb 2026 · 45 min · Night, City streets, Rain Supervisor: Mom Notes: Left turns across traffic on Main St. Better than last week. Still braking late in the wet.

More examples and a field-by-field breakdown are in the driving log template guide.

Paper or app?

Both work. They fail differently, which is the useful way to choose.

Paper logPhone app
Always with youOnly if it lives in the right carYes — the phone comes on every drive
Running totalYou add it up, repeatedly, and get it wrongCalculated, always current
Night subtotalA second column you also add upTagged and totalled automatically
Can be lostYes, permanentlyOnly with the device — share a copy occasionally
Parent signatureDirectly on the sheetSignature goes on the state form you copy onto
Accepted as a state formYes, where the state publishes the sheetNo — it is a record you copy from

The honest recommendation for most families: log on the phone, print or transcribe at the end. The phone captures the data accurately because it is in the car; the state form gets filled in once, from a total that is already correct. If your state mandates a specific sheet — Ohio, Pennsylvania, New York, Massachusetts all do — you were going to need the form anyway.

Habits that keep a log honest

  • Log in the driveway. Before the seatbelt is off. Attach it to an existing habit and it stops being a task.
  • Decide who owns the log. One person, not "whoever remembers". Two people casually logging the same drives is how totals get double-counted.
  • Count the boring trips. School runs, errands, the drive to practice. They are the majority of your hours and the ones people forget to claim.
  • Check the night subtotal monthly. Night is the requirement that quietly falls behind, and it is much easier to fix in November than in June.
  • Send a copy to a parent once a month. That is your backup, and it keeps the certifying adult's number and the teen's number in agreement.
  • Never round up to a milestone. Forty-seven hours is forty-seven hours. The number on a signed certification should be one you would repeat under oath, because in several states that is literally what you are doing.

What to do about gaps

Everyone has one. Here is how to close it without lying.

  1. Fix the date range. Work out when you stopped logging and when you started again.
  2. Rebuild from evidence, not vibes. Calendars, texts, photos, the supervising adult's memory of specific trips. "We drove to the tournament on the 14th, that was about 40 minutes each way."
  3. Enter the drives you can substantiate, individually where you can, consolidated where you cannot — "March 2026, roughly 8 hours, city streets".
  4. Annotate every reconstructed entry. "Reconstructed from calendar, logged 6 Aug 2026." Nobody has ever been penalised for a note explaining where a number came from.
  5. Leave out what you genuinely cannot support. Undercounting slightly is a much better problem than a certification you cannot defend.

Frequently asked questions

What information do I need to record for each drive?

At minimum: the date, how long you drove, and whether it was at night. Most states ask for a total and a night subtotal, so those two numbers are the ones that have to be right. Beyond that, record conditions (highway, rain, city, parking, heavy traffic), who supervised, and a one-line note about what you practiced. The extra detail costs ten seconds and turns a column of numbers into something you can actually plan from.

How do I handle a gap where I forgot to log?

Reconstruct it honestly and annotate it. Work backwards from your calendar, texts, and the supervising adult's memory — "we drove to the game on the 14th, that was about 40 minutes" — and enter the drives with a note saying they were reconstructed. Do not invent a round number to paper over the gap. A parent signature on a state certification form is a legal attestation, and a log with an honest annotation is worth more than a tidy one that isn't true.

Do I have to log every short trip?

You should, and it is the biggest thing people get wrong. A twelve-minute drive to school is twelve minutes toward the requirement. Families who only log "real practice sessions" routinely undercount by twenty or thirty hours across the permit period, then spend the final month driving in circles to make up hours they had already earned.

Does my state require a specific log format?

Some do. Ohio requires the Fifty-Hour Affidavit (BMV 5791), Pennsylvania form DL-180C, New York form MV-262, and Massachusetts publishes an official RMV Supervised Driving Log. Others, California among them, simply recommend keeping a log in any format. Check the accepted format with your licensing agency before your road test — and note that even where a state form is required, you still need a running record to fill it in from.

Is a phone app better than paper?

For most families, yes — mainly because the phone is already in the car and the paper sheet is in the other car. A digital log also totals itself, which removes the arithmetic errors that make a paper log unreliable. Paper wins in two situations: when your state requires a specific printed form signed by a parent, and when the teen does not carry a phone. Many families do both: log on the phone, transcribe onto the state form at the end.

When should I log a drive?

In the driveway, before you go inside. Every minute you wait makes the entry less accurate, and "I'll do it later" is how logs die. It takes about fifteen seconds. Make it part of parking the car, like taking the keys out.
