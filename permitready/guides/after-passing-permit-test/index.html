You Passed the Permit Test — Here's What Comes Next

The test was the short part. What follows is a waiting period, a stack of supervised hours, and a road test you can only book once both are done.

TechSmog Apps › PermitReady › Guides › After Passing the Permit Test

Passing the permit test earns you a learner's permit, not a license. From here you need to hold that permit for a minimum period — usually 6 months, 12 in Florida — and complete your state's supervised driving hours, most commonly 50 total with 10 at night. Both have to be finished before you can take the road test.

  1. You'll receive your learner's permit

    Usually the same day. Many states hand over a temporary paper permit at the counter and mail the plastic card within a couple of weeks; the paper one is valid, so do not wait for the card to start driving.

    What the permit lets you do: drive on public roads with a qualified supervising driver in the front passenger seat. What it does not let you do: drive alone, ever, for any distance, including moving the car in a driveway onto a public street.

    Who qualifies as a supervisor varies more than people expect. Most states say a licensed driver 21 or older. California requires 25 (or a licensed parent, guardian, spouse, or employer). Massachusetts requires 21 plus at least one year of licensed driving experience. Check yours before your first drive, because hours logged with an unqualified supervisor do not count.

    Several states also restrict when a permit holder may drive. Florida limits new permit holders to daylight hours for the first three months, then until 10 PM. New York applies different rules in New York City, on Long Island, and in Westchester. These restrictions shape how quickly you can accumulate hours — especially night hours.

  2. The supervised hours requirement

    Every state with graduated licensing requires a block of supervised practice before a teen can be licensed. Most land between 40 and 65 hours; roughly half require exactly 50. Almost all set a separate night minimum, usually 10 hours.

    The requirement exists because crash risk for newly licensed drivers is concentrated in the first months of solo driving, and supervised practice is the only thing shown to reduce it. Fifty hours is not an arbitrary bureaucratic number — it is roughly the point at which the basics stop consuming attention and a driver can start noticing the things that actually cause crashes.

    Find your state's exact figure in the state-by-state hours guide, and check what counts as a loggable hour before you assume parking-lot practice or pre-permit driving will help.

  3. Start logging immediately

    On the first drive, not the fifth. Three reasons, in ascending order of importance:

    • Accuracy. Nobody remembers whether the Tuesday drive in March was 25 minutes or 45. A log reconstructed in the last week before a road test is a guess with a signature on it, and a parent is the one signing.
    • Visibility. A running total tells you in month two that you are behind, when there is still time to fix it. Discovering it in month five means a scramble.
    • Night hours. See below. They are the constraint, and they are invisible until you are counting them.

    What to record per drive: date, start and end time (or duration), conditions — night, highway, rain, city, heavy traffic — and who supervised. That level of detail survives any question about how your state defines "night," and it is what an official state form asks for anyway.

  4. Night hours — don't leave them to the last minute

    This is the single most common way a licensing timeline slips. The total hours accumulate on their own, because you are already driving to school and to the store. Night hours do not: they require someone to deliberately get in a car after dark.

    Three complications make it worse than it sounds:

    • Definitions differ. New York counts sunset to sunrise and requires 15 hours. California counts only 11 PM to 5 AM. A 7 PM December drive is a night hour in one state and not the other.
    • Summer is brutal. In June, sunset in the northern half of the country is after 8:30 PM. A family logging hours over a summer can hit 50 total and 2 at night.
    • Permit restrictions can block them. If your state limits permit driving to before 10 PM, and your night window does not open until 11 PM, you have a scheduling problem that needs solving early.

    The fix is boring and effective: bank night hours in winter, and make one evening drive a week non-negotiable from the start.

  5. The road test

    To schedule it, you generally need all of the following in place:

    • The minimum holding period elapsed — 6 months in most states, 12 in Florida, counted from the permit issue date.
    • The supervised hours complete, including the night minimum and any weather-condition hours your state requires (Pennsylvania wants 5 in bad weather; South Dakota wants 10 in inclement conditions).
    • The log or certification signed by a parent or guardian, on your state's form where one exists — Massachusetts publishes a log PDF, New York uses form MV-262.
    • Any required driver education completed, with the certificate.
    • The minimum age for a provisional or junior license in your state.
    • A road-legal vehicle, registered and insured, with working lights and signals. Tests get cancelled over a burnt-out brake light more often than anyone would like.

    Book early. Road test appointments in busy metros are routinely scheduled weeks out, and the appointment is worth reserving as soon as you can see the finish line.

A realistic timeline

For a typical 50-hour, 10-night, 6-month state, starting the week the permit is issued:

  • Months 1–2: short, low-stakes drives. Quiet streets, then busier ones. Roughly 1–2 hours a week is normal and fine. Start the night hours now — even 20 minutes after dark counts.
  • Months 3–4: the school run becomes the practice. Add highway driving deliberately. You should be past 25 hours and at 5 or more night hours by the end of month 4.
  • Month 5: the conditions you have been avoiding — rain, heavy traffic, parallel parking, unfamiliar routes. Confirm your state's exact requirements and get the official form.
  • Month 6: finish the total, get the log signed, book the test. Practice the specific maneuvers your state's road test scores.

Frequently asked questions

What happens right after I pass the permit test?

You are issued a learner's permit, often as a temporary paper permit with the card arriving by mail. It starts both your minimum holding period and your supervised hours.

Can I drive alone with a learner's permit?

No. A qualified supervising driver must be in the front passenger seat at all times. The age requirement varies — usually 21, but 25 in California and 21-plus-one-year in Massachusetts.

How many supervised hours will I need?

Most states require 40 to 65 hours with at least 10 at night; about half require 50. Check the state-by-state guide for your exact figure.

When should I start logging?

The first drive. Hours count from the permit issue date, and a log reconstructed months later is a guess someone has to sign.

How long until I can take the road test?

Usually 6 months from the permit issue date, 12 in Florida, and only once the supervised hours are complete. Both conditions have to be met.

What do I bring to the road test?

Your permit, the signed log or certification, proof of the holding period, identity and residency documents, any required driver education certificate, and a registered, insured, road-legal vehicle. Check your agency's checklist before booking.
